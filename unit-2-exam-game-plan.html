Solving polynomials

3–4 problems

How he'll ask it

"List all possible rational zeros of f(x) = …"

"Solve the equation … given that 4 is a root."

"Find all zeros of f(x) = …"

Your answer must have

  • The ± list of \(\tfrac pq\) (when asked)
  • The zero you tested and your division
  • Every zero listed at the end

Steps

  1. \(p\) = factors of the last number, \(q\) = factors of the first number. List every \(\pm\tfrac pq\).
  2. Plug those in until one gives 0.
  3. Long divide by that factor.
  4. Factor what's left, or use the quadratic formula.

Book #55: \(f(x)=6x^3+x^2-4x+1\)

\(\tfrac pq:\ \pm1,\ \pm\tfrac12,\ \pm\tfrac13,\ \pm\tfrac16\)

\(f(-1)=-6+1+4+1=0\ \checkmark\)

Divide by \(x+1\): \(6x^2-5x+1=(3x-1)(2x-1)\)

Zeros: \(-1,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac12\)

Book #48: Solve \(x^3-17x+4=0\) given that 4 is a root.

Divide by \(x-4\): \(x^2+4x-1\)

Quadratic formula: \(x=\dfrac{-4\pm\sqrt{16+4}}{2}=-2\pm\sqrt5\)

\(\{4,\ -2+\sqrt5,\ -2-\sqrt5\}\)

Polynomial and rational inequalities

2–3 problems

How he'll ask it

"Solve each inequality and graph the solution set on a real number line. Express the solution set in interval notation."

Your answer must have

  • A number line with dots and shading
  • Interval notation

Steps

  1. Get 0 on one side. With a fraction, combine into one fraction.
  2. Find the boundary points: zeros of the top and zeros of the bottom.
  3. Test one number in each section for + or −.
  4. Keep the sections that match the sign you want.
  5. \(\le\) or \(\ge\): closed dot on the top's zeros. The bottom's zeros are always open.

Book #89: \(\dfrac{(x+1)(x-2)}{x-1}\ge0\)

Boundaries: \(-1\) (closed), \(1\) (open, it's on the bottom), \(2\) (closed)

Signs: \(-\ \ |\ \ +\ \ |\ \ -\ \ |\ \ +\)

\([-1,\,1)\cup[2,\,\infty)\)

Practice test #28: \(\dfrac{2x+1}{x-3}\le3\)

Move the 3 over: \(\dfrac{2x+1-3(x-3)}{x-3}=\dfrac{-x+10}{x-3}\le0\)

Boundaries: \(10\) (closed), \(3\) (open)

\((-\infty,\,3)\cup[10,\,\infty)\)

Asymptotes and holes

2–3 problems

How he'll ask it

"Find the vertical asymptotes, if any, the horizontal asymptote, if one exists, and the slant asymptote, if there is one." His sheet adds holes.

Your answer must have

  • Four labeled lines: VA, HA, Slant, Hole
  • "None" written on any that don't exist

Steps

  1. Factor the top and bottom.
  2. A factor that cancels is a hole. Plug its \(x\) into the simplified function to get the \(y\).
  3. Bottom zeros left over are the VAs.
  4. Compare degrees: top smaller gives \(y=0\); equal gives leading numbers over each other; top bigger gives no HA.
  5. Top exactly one degree bigger: long divide. The quotient is the slant asymptote.

Example: \(f(x)=\dfrac{3x^2-12}{x^2-x-2}=\dfrac{3(x-2)(x+2)}{(x-2)(x+1)}\)

\(x-2\) cancels, leaving \(\dfrac{3(x+2)}{x+1}\). At \(x=2\): \(\dfrac{12}{3}=4\)

VA \(x=-1\) · HA \(y=3\) · Slant: none · Hole \((2,4)\)

Book #76: \(y=\dfrac{x^2+2x-3}{x-3}\)

Long divide: \(x+5\) with remainder 12

VA \(x=3\) · HA none · Slant \(y=x+5\) · Hole: none

Imaginary numbers

2–3 problems

How he'll ask it

"Perform the indicated operations and write the result in standard form."

"Solve using the quadratic formula. Express solutions in standard form."

Your answer must have

  • The form \(a+bi\), with no \(i\) and no square root on the bottom

Steps

  1. Change \(\sqrt{-n}\) to \(i\sqrt n\) first, before anything else.
  2. FOIL, then change \(i^2\) to \(-1\).
  3. To divide, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate (flip the middle sign). This is "rationalizing the denominator."
  4. Split into \(a+bi\).

Book #7: \(\dfrac{3+4i}{4-2i}\cdot\dfrac{4+2i}{4+2i}=\dfrac{12+6i+16i+8i^2}{16+4}=\dfrac{4+22i}{20}\)

\(\tfrac15+\tfrac{11}{10}i\)

Book #9: \((-2+\sqrt{-100})^2=(-2+10i)^2=4-40i+100i^2\)

\(-96-40i\)

Domain of a rational function

1–2 problems

How he'll ask it

"Find the domain of each rational function."

Your answer must have

  • Interval notation that skips every \(x\) that makes the bottom 0

Steps

  1. Set the bottom equal to 0 and solve.
  2. Leave those values out. Do this before canceling, because holes are left out too.

\(f(x)=\dfrac{x}{x^2-16}\): \(x^2-16=0\Rightarrow x=\pm4\)

\((-\infty,-4)\cup(-4,4)\cup(4,\infty)\)

\(f(x)=\dfrac{4x^2}{x^2+3}\): \(x^2+3\) is never 0

\((-\infty,\infty)\)

Writing a polynomial from its roots

1–2 problems

How he'll ask it

"Find an nth-degree polynomial function with real coefficients satisfying the given conditions."

Your answer must have

  • A final \(f(x)=\ldots\) multiplied all the way out

Steps

  1. Every complex zero brings its conjugate: \(2-3i\) means \(2+3i\) too.
  2. Write \(f(x)=a(x-r_1)(x-r_2)\cdots\)
  3. Plug in the given point to find \(a\).
  4. Multiply it all out.

Book #61: \(n=3\); 2 and \(2-3i\) are zeros; \(f(1)=-10\)

\((x-(2-3i))(x-(2+3i))=(x-2)^2+9=x^2-4x+13\)

\(f(x)=a(x-2)(x^2-4x+13)\). Then \(f(1)=a(-1)(10)=-10\), so \(a=1\)

\(f(x)=x^3-6x^2+21x-26\)

Zeros, multiplicity and the graph

1–2 problems

How he'll ask it

"Find the zeros and give the multiplicity of each zero. State whether the graph crosses the x-axis, or touches the x-axis and turns around, at each zero."

"Use the Leading Coefficient Test to determine the graph's end behavior."

Your answer must have

  • One line per zero: the zero, its multiplicity, and "crosses" or "touches and turns around"
  • One end-behavior sentence

Rules

  • Odd multiplicity crosses. Even multiplicity touches and turns around.
DegreeLeading coefficientEnd behavior
OddPositiveFalls left, rises right
OddNegativeRises left, falls right
EvenPositiveRises left, rises right
EvenNegativeFalls left, falls right

\(x=1\): multiplicity 1, crosses

\(x=-2\): multiplicity 2, touches and turns around

\(x=-5\): multiplicity 3, crosses

Degree \(1+2+3=6\) is even and \(-2\) is negative.

End behavior: falls left, falls right

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

1–2 problems

How he'll ask it

"Find all the zeros of each polynomial function and write the polynomial as a product of linear factors."

Your answer must have

  • All zeros, including the \(i\) ones. A degree-\(n\) polynomial has \(n\) zeros.
  • \(f(x)\) written as factors like \((x-r)\), including \((x-i)(x+i)\)

Book #63: \(f(x)=2x^4+3x^3+3x-2\)

\(\tfrac12\) and \(-2\) both give 0. Dividing them out leaves \(x^2+1\), so \(x=\pm i\).

Zeros: \(\tfrac12,\ -2,\ i,\ -i\)

\(f(x)=(2x-1)(x+2)(x-i)(x+i)\)
